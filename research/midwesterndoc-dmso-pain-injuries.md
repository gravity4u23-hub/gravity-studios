## What the article says

The article is part two of a series on DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). Its argument: DMSO is a remarkably safe, highly effective painkiller and healer of musculoskeletal injuries, and the FDA suppressed it after a 1965 research halt despite decades of positive evidence.

**Mechanisms it proposes (with cited studies):**
- Selective blockade of small pain fibers (C-fibers first, then A-delta), shown in cat and frog nerve studies at 5–15% concentrations; the block reverses when DMSO is washed off.
- Suppression of NMDA and AMPA receptor signalling and of sodium/calcium entry into cells — the same classes of mechanism proposed for its effect on complex regional pain syndrome and cancer pain.
- Acetylcholinesterase inhibition (weaker than pharmaceutical inhibitors, so no cholinergic syndrome seen), which the author links to increased parasympathetic activity.
- Anti-inflammatory action: reduced inflammatory cytokines and prostaglandins, free-radical scavenging, reduced edema, improved blood flow to poorly perfused tissue (including joints).
- Muscle relaxation: topical application produced electromyographic evidence of muscle relaxation within an hour; a 1966 study found muscles in spasm relaxed within 60 minutes.
- Blood-stasis resolution: the author argues chronic pain often comes from insufficient blood reaching an area, and DMSO restores flow.

**Evidence it presents:**
- Mostly 1960s uncontrolled studies and case series, plus a smaller number of later controlled trials. The largest datasets: a 1967 analysis of 76 researchers' studies covering 1,900 patients (72% improved), a 1965–67 run of studies on bursitis, arthritis and sprains, and a symposium dataset of 9,521 patients.
- Condition-specific sections: headaches (75%+ response in one 59-patient series, 26 of 35 trigeminal neuralgia patients improved), fibromyalgia (70% benefit claimed by the DMSO pioneer, no formal literature), spinal problems (one 1968 study of 38 disc patients where DMSO halved treatment time, plus extensive congressional-hearing testimonies), CRPS (multiple small trials at 50% DMSO, including a 2003 randomized trial of 64 vs 67 patients against NAC), post-surgical pain, wound healing and scars (one large chronic-wound study reporting 95% complete recovery), rheumatoid arthritis (several 1960s–80s studies, 44–84% response rates), and sports injuries.
- Animal work: horses (bursitis/synovitis ~90% improved in 50+ cases), dogs (ACL recovery halved), rabbits (41% less ankle stiffness after fracture), newts (limb regeneration sped by 2–3 days).

**History it tells:**
- DMSO spread rapidly in the early 1960s; a March 1966 New York Academy of Sciences symposium drew over 1,000 researchers and 82 papers.
- On November 10, 1965, the FDA issued a halt on DMSO clinical research, which the author calls a global research ban. Research largely stopped; an underground market followed.
- A 60 Minutes segment aired shortly before a March 24, 1980 congressional hearing that questioned the FDA's handling of DMSO; a Senate subcommittee hearing followed on July 31, 1980. Former Indiana governor Otis Bowen used DMSO for his wife's terminal myeloma pain, denounced the FDA embargo publicly, and later became Reagan's Secretary of Health and Human Services (1985) — without DMSO's status changing.
- The article notes DMSO's sole FDA-approved human use (interstitial cystitis bladder instillation) and its approved use as a vehicle in topical diclofenac products, plus full acceptance in veterinary medicine.

**Sports section:**
- Atlanta Falcons quarterback June Jones became a prominent DMSO advocate and testified to Congress; a Dallas Cowboys lineman described its use publicly in 2013.
- The former team physician for the Oakland Raiders testified he used 70% topical DMSO on a controlled basis, 20–30 times a year for 5 years, mostly in the first 3–4 days of acute injury with severe swelling; he reported good-to-excellent results 70–80% of the time and players returning 50–75% faster. He did not find it helpful for chronic injuries.
- Podiatrist Lowell Scott Weil, physician for the Chicago Bears and US Olympic gymnastics team, reported a 60% success rate over 12 years, best for tendinitis, myositis, sprains and strains; only side effect seen was skin irritation.
- A 1967 paper on 8 Buffalo Bills players reported return to full duty 50% sooner; a 1966 study of 28 professional baseball players found downtime one-third of the previous year's.

## Home-use protocols — what this article actually contains

**Important: this article does NOT contain the full home protocols.** It ends by promising them in a final part ("I will discuss the established protocols for applying topical DMSO... key precautions... tricks... and our preferred sources"). That later part is a separate piece of the series. What this article does give, scattered through the studies and notes:

- **Concentrations used in the cited studies:** 50% for CRPS trials; 70% for the Raiders physician and for most topical use; 90% in several early studies; 10% gel in a blinded acute-injury study; 25% gel in an osteoarthritis RCT.
- **Acute injuries:** often applied every 2–3 hours; broader area covered and more DMSO used tends to work better; the author warns pain relief can mask a serious injury (the Merck memo advised X-ray first to rule out fracture).
- **Chronic pain:** typically takes 4–7 days of applications to begin working and 6–8 weeks for lasting relief; about a quarter of chronic patients in one study had a delayed response.
- **Where it works best topically:** pain above the waist and smaller joints; hips respond least to topical application — the DMSO pioneer used injections rather than topical for hips and knees, though the author says knees frequently respond to topical use.
- **Systemic effect:** one study found 65% of patients got relief when DMSO was applied at the pain site and 61.5% got comparable relief when applied elsewhere on the body.
- **General shape of the author's approach (from the series' companion handbook summary, secondary source):** start at a lower concentration (30–50%) and work up as tolerated; 70% is often described as the usual strength; clean the skin first; let it dry ~20 minutes before putting clothing over it.

No dosage for any individual is given in this file, and none should be taken from it. See Integration.

## Safety warnings in the article

- **Wash the skin first.** DMSO carries anything on the skin into the body — the article names pesticides, nicotine and mercury as examples of things it can draw in.
- **Pregnancy:** the article says there are unresolved questions about using DMSO while pregnant, to be discussed in an upcoming article.
- **Allergic reaction:** the article puts the rate at about 1 in 2,000 people and says it can be screened for.
- **Skin irritation:** described as the most common side effect, affecting 50–75% of users at 70% topical concentration, reversible and mitigable; garlic-like odor lasting hours is the other near-universal effect.
- **Masking:** pain relief can mask a fracture or serious injury; the author repeats the Merck memo's advice to get an X-ray first for acute trauma.
- **Sensitive patients:** fibromyalgia patients sometimes need a slow start or the initial detoxification response is too much.

**Safety context the article does not stress (from independent sources):** the systematic review below records dose-related effects including hypotension, bradycardia, dyspnea, headache, sedation and dizziness, mostly with IV/high-dose use; MSK notes a case of recurrent cerebral hemorrhage in a man using DMSO and neural damage in mice (clinical relevance unknown), and advises pregnant women to avoid it. Industrial-grade DMSO may contain benzene, acetone or other contaminants — only pharmaceutical/USP grade is made for human contact (Mayo Clinic).

### a. "Banned for 60 years" — MISLEADING

The FDA halted DMSO clinical research on November 10, 1965 — the article itself dates it. But "banned" overstates the reality in three ways: DMSO remained legal throughout as an industrial solvent and as a veterinary drug; the FDA approved it for human use in 1978 as RIMSO-50, a 50% sterile solution instilled into the bladder for interstitial cystitis, and that remains its only FDA-approved human use (Healthline, Mayo Clinic, ConsumerLab); and DMSO is also an approved excipient/vehicle in topical diclofenac products (Pennsaid). The accurate statement is narrower: never approved for pain or musculoskeletal use in humans, and clinical research was halted in 1965. "Banned" is the post's word, not the regulatory reality.

### b. "Over 4000 studies" — MISLEADING

PubMed returns ~28,000 results for "dimethyl sulfoxide" and ~42,000 for "DMSO" — but the overwhelming majority mention DMSO as a laboratory solvent, cryoprotectant or drug vehicle, not as a therapy. The number of studies actually testing DMSO as a treatment for musculoskeletal injury or chronic pain is in the dozens to low hundreds; the article itself cites roughly 200 references, mostly from the 1960s. The "4,000" figure appears to come from a different count: a Merck memo cited in the article covered 4,000 *patients*, and DMSO pioneer Stanley Jacob is on record saying over 40,000 articles exist on DMSO's chemistry and ~11,000 on its medical implications. So a large literature mentioning DMSO is real; a literature of 4,000 studies *showing it treats* these conditions is not.

### c. "Never been linked to a single death" — PARTIAL

No death has been proven caused by DMSO alone in the published literature — that core is defensible. But the absolute wording fails: a 2023 published case report (PMC) describes a 37-year-old woman who died after IV infusions of DMSO plus hydrogen peroxide at an alternative-medicine clinic; DMSO was the initial suspect, and the autopsy ultimately blamed the hydrogen peroxide, not the DMSO. Deaths have also been reported in leukemia patients receiving DMSO-containing cryopreserved stem-cell infusions (abdominal pain, nausea, bradycardia, "even death" in the cited review), though those are confounded by the underlying disease and the infusion itself. Honest version: no death attributed to DMSO alone as the sole cause; DMSO has been suspected in at least one fatal case and is present in fatal infusion settings.

### d. NSAIDs and opioids — PARTIAL (split)

- **Opioids: VERIFIED.** CDC/NCHS final data: 79,358 opioid-involved overdose deaths in 2023 and 54,045 in 2024 — tens of thousands a year, even after the 2024 decline.
- **NSAIDs: PARTIAL.** The classic estimate is ~16,500 US deaths a year from NSAID-related GI complications (Wolfe et al., 1999, ARAMIS data) — that is "tens of thousands" only at the low end, and it is a 1999 figure. Later work puts it lower: a 2005 American Journal of Gastroenterology editorial notes mortality around 30% of the popularized rate, and a 1998 estimate counted ~3,300 deaths a year among the elderly. The direction of the claim (NSAIDs kill thousands yearly) is right; the "tens of thousands" framing leans on the oldest, highest estimate.

### e. Raiders trainer, a decade, every time — PARTIAL (video corrects the article)

The video transcript settles this one — and it backs the post, not the article. The man on camera says, in his own words, that he was a certified athletic trainer with the Raiders from 1991 to about 1999, working under head trainer George Anderson (a trainer there since the 1960s, who had already used DMSO on many injuries before the speaker arrived). His first major case was a severe ankle sprain to star center Don Mosebar, five or six days before a big game: about 80% of the swelling and about half the bruising gone within a day and a half, Mosebar practiced the day before the game, played, did not re-injure the ankle, and had no long-term effects. A second case was a defensive back prone to hamstring strains. His summary phrase is that DMSO "worked every time to some degree" — sometimes 20%, sometimes 80% — for sprains and contusions.

So the post's core is the trainer's own account: trainer (not physician), 1991–1999, 80% of swelling gone in ~1.5 days. The article's version — former team *physician*, 5 years, 70–80% good-to-excellent — appears to be a separate account (a different person testifying), not the man in the video. What the post still gets wrong: it drops the trainer's own qualifier ("to some degree"), rounds ~8 years up to "a decade," and the "no side effects" line is the post's addition — the clip mentions no side effects either way, and the article itself lists skin irritation. The thumbnail's "Jonathan Jones" name is unverified: the speaker never states his name on camera, and no public record connects a Jonathan Jones to Raiders DMSO use; the article's named NFL advocate is a different Jones (June Jones, Atlanta Falcons quarterback).

### f. 60 Minutes + March 24, 1980 hearing — VERIFIED

Mike Wallace presented DMSO on 60 Minutes in 1980 — March 23, 1980 per Midwestern Doctor's own earlier post (some secondary sources say March 28), with a second segment July 6, 1980. The hearing "DMSO, new hope for arthritis?" before the House Select Committee on Aging, 96th Congress, 2nd session, March 24, 1980, is catalogued in the University of Pennsylvania's Online Books Page (U.S. Government Printing Office, 1980); the Senate Subcommittee on Health and Scientific Research hearing of July 31, 1980 is likewise catalogued. The "grilled the FDA" framing and the claim the FDA promised fair treatment are the author's characterizations — the hearings themselves are documented fact.

### g. Systematic review, minor/transient side effects — VERIFIED (with dose qualifiers)

Madsen et al., "Adverse reactions of dimethyl sulfoxide in humans: a systematic review," F1000Research 2018 (v2, 2019): 109 human studies. Gastrointestinal and skin reactions were the commonest; most reactions were transient without need for intervention; a dose relationship was seen; the authors concluded DMSO "seems to be safe to use in small doses." Memorial Sloan Kettering cites the same review. The qualifier matters: the review also records rarer, more serious events at higher doses and IV use (hypotension, bradycardia, dyspnea, headache, sedation, rare seizures/encephalopathy), so "only minor/transient" holds for small topical doses, not for all routes and doses.

### h. 90-day study at 3–30x dose, no toxicity — PARTIAL

The study exists as described in its source: Richard Brobyn's "The Human Toxicology of Dimethyl Sulfoxide" (dmso.org) describes the Vacaville prison-hospital studies (1967–68), 80% DMSO gel at 1 g/kg/day by daily dermal application — estimated 3 to 30 times the human treatment dose — with no significant toxicity in the battery of blood and urine tests (eosinophilia excepted). Caveats: this is an unpublished report hosted by an advocacy site, not a peer-reviewed journal paper; it was conducted on prisoners, which would not pass modern ethics review; and the FDA's stated safety concern at the time was lens changes seen in animals, which the human studies did not reproduce. Real study, accurately described, weak provenance.

### i. 80–90% success rate — PARTIAL

The article's own cited figures cluster below the headline: 72% improved in the 1967 analysis of 1,900 patients; 70–80% good-to-excellent (Raiders physician); 60% (Weil, 12 years); 84% pain relief (Steinberg, chronic arthritis); 77% subjective relief (RA). The 80–90% figure is the author's summary synthesis, sitting at the top end of his own cited range — and nearly all the underlying studies are uncontrolled 1960s case series, not modern RCTs. The one modern-ish RCTs cited are smaller and mixed (e.g., 25% DMSO gel beat placebo for knee OA pain but below the threshold of clinical meaningfulness in one trial; a 2008 systematic review of DMSO/MSM for OA found no definitive evidence). Direction (most patients in these old series improved) is supported; the precise 80–90% is the author's rounding up.

## Takeaways

1. **The core story is real but the numbers are inflated at every step — with one big correction from the video.** The 1960s DMSO literature, the 1965 FDA research halt, the 1980 60 Minutes segments and congressional hearings are all documented. On the Raiders story the VIDEO backs the post, not the article: the man on camera is a Raiders certified athletic trainer (1991–1999) telling the Don Mosebar ankle case in the post's own numbers — 80% of swelling gone in about a day and a half. The article's physician/5-year/70–80% version looks like a separate account. The post still drops the trainer's own qualifier ("worked every time to some degree"), rounds 8 years to "a decade," and "banned for 60 years" is still the wrong word for a research halt. Read the article AND the video, not the post alone.
2. **The evidence base is old and mostly uncontrolled.** The strongest material is 1960s case series and a handful of small controlled trials (CRPS at 50% DMSO is the best-controlled corner). Modern systematic reviews of DMSO for osteoarthritis find no definitive evidence. This is a "promising but unproven by modern standards" picture, not a settled one.
3. **The safety picture is genuinely favorable at small topical doses — with real limits.** The 109-study systematic review supports "mostly mild and transient" at low doses. The honest risks are skin irritation, garlic odor, dose-related systemic effects, the carrier effect (anything on the skin goes in), pregnancy (unresolved), and grade (industrial DMSO can carry benzene/acetone). The "never linked to a single death" line is too absolute.
4. **The article does not contain the home protocols it promises.** They are deferred to a later part of the series. What it gives is study concentrations and timing patterns (see above) — useful as background, not a protocol.
5. **The comparison drug deaths are the strongest part of the case.** Opioid deaths (54k–79k/year, CDC) and the classic NSAID estimate (~16.5k/year) make the toxicity contrast real, even if the NSAID number leans on a 1999 estimate.

**What is usable:**
- The carrier-effect warning is the most actionable safety item for his current experiments: DMSO pulls whatever is on the skin into the body, so the skin must be clean first and nothing else (no lotions, no magnesium oil residue he hasn't accounted for, no nicotine) should be on the application area. This matters more than any concentration question.
- The grade rule: only pharmaceutical/USP-grade DMSO is made for human contact; industrial solvent grade can carry benzene or acetone through the skin. If what he has came from a feed store or hardware-style source, that is the first thing to check.
- The timing pattern for chronic pain (days to begin working, weeks for lasting relief) sets expectations: a single application that does nothing proves little, and the article's own data says chronic cases can look unresponsive at first.
- The topical-above-the-waist note cuts both ways for spine pain: the article claims good results for cervical/lumbar disc problems topically, but also says larger joints and the hips respond least to topical use.

**Safety gaps to flag honestly:**
- No FDA approval for this use; the only approved human use is bladder instillation for interstitial cystitis. Everything here is off-label.
- Pregnancy questions are unresolved (not directly applicable, but the author's own caveat).
- The 1-in-2,000 allergy figure and the skin-irritation rate (50–75% at 70%) come from the author's own sources, not from a modern trial.
- No dosage is invented or recommended in this file. The concentrations listed are what studies used, not a prescription for him.
