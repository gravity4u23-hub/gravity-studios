# Loophole Hunt — $1M whole-life policy loan via EIN + trust (sirenpatriot video)

Date: Nov 21, 2026. Hunt commissioned by the owner after the first test refuted the claim 0/6.
Mission: find ANY legal mechanism by which the video's play works — or the closest working version.
Method: 16 targeted web searches + page fetches across all 8 angles below. No numbers invented; where a figure is my arithmetic, the assumptions are stated.

## Verdict

**No loophole found that produces $1M now from zero income and zero capital.** Every angle was tested; every one closes on the same wall, and the wall is not a rule that can be routed around — it is arithmetic: a new $1M policy on a healthy young person is worth ~$0 (in fact slightly less than $0) to every party in the chain, because the buyer/lender would have to pay ~$900–1,150/mo in premiums for ~45 years to eventually collect $1M.

But the hunt was not empty. It found:
- The exact lowest bars for every adjacent product (including one premium-finance variant that drops the net-worth floor to ~$1M and income to $100k — still out of reach from zero, but the lowest real bar that exists).
- One third-party lender that explicitly answers the face-value question with a "No."
- The one genuine grain of truth in the video, and the closest working versions, ranked.

## Angle 1 — EIN + trust: what does it actually unlock?

**What an EIN does:** It is a free tax ID from the IRS. It lets a trust open a bank account in the trust's name, file taxes, and be identified to institutions. It creates no money, no credit, and no borrowing power. Nothing in tax law ties an EIN to funds.

**Can a trust borrow?** Yes — but only like any borrower, and only against real collateral:
- A revocable/living trust is treated by banks like the individual who created it; the trustee signs, and the bank underwrites the trustee's credit and the collateral.
- An irrevocable trust is much harder: most conventional lenders won't lend to one at all. Fannie Mae only buys loans made to natural persons, revocable trusts, and certain land trusts — irrevocable trusts are excluded, so banks that sell to the secondary market can't lend to them. Only specialty/private lenders will, and only secured by trust-owned real estate.
- Specialty trust lenders (e.g., North Coast Financial) lend 65–75% LTV against trust-owned **real estate** — the trust must own property with equity. Proceeds go to the trust's bank account.
- Requirements everywhere: trust documents must authorize borrowing; financial statements; collateral with sufficient equity; usually a personal guarantee from a human the lender can hold accountable.

**Applied to the video:** the trust's only asset is a life policy with ~$0 cash value. No real estate, no income, no financial statements, no personal guarantee worth anything. No lender in this chain lends against a policy's face value. The trust step adds paperwork, not money.

**What the EIN + trust DOES unlock (honest list):** a bank account in the trust's name; a tax ID; the ability to own the policy so the death benefit stays out of the estate (the ILIT's real purpose, relevant only above the ~$15M estate-tax exemption); and the ability to start building a business credit file — see Angle 7.

Sources: northcoastfinancialinc.com "Can a Trust Get a Mortgage or Loan?"; legalclarity.org "Can an Irrevocable Trust Get a Loan?"; consultingtimes.com "Can a trust borrow money from a bank?"

## Angle 2 — Challenge: does ANY lender lend against FACE value / death benefit while alive?

Tested: policy-loan firms, third-party lenders, insurance-backed lines of credit, collateral-assignment loans.

**The direct answer, from a lender that sells exactly this product:**
Kensington Financial Associates (kensingtonfa.com), a third-party policy lender — FAQ: **"Will you loan against my death benefit? A: No — we only lend against the guaranteed cash value of your policy, up to 95%."** Its borrowing base "may not exceed 95% of the guaranteed cash value at any time during the term of your loan." It also advertises "no income verification for loans up to $1 million" — but that is a loan *of* up to $1M against cash value, which requires ~$1.05M of cash value first.

**Insurance-Backed Line of Credit (IBLOC), The Bancorp Bank:** "lets you tap up to 95% of the cash value of eligible whole life insurance policies." Minimum credit line $75,000. Policy must be in effect at least 12 months. Subject to credit approval and underwriting. Collateral Lending Value = cash surrender value × up to 95%. Eligible carriers limited (Guardian, MassMutual, Northwestern Mutual, NY Life, John Hancock, Penn Mutual, Ameritas, Security Mutual).

**Insurer policy loans:** 75–90% of cash surrender value, per every source in the first test and this one (Hettler Insurance: "Most insurers allow loans up to 75%–90% of cash value").

**Collateral assignment (bank loan secured by the policy):** the lender gets a conditional claim on the death benefit *if you die or default* — but the loan amount is set by normal underwriting of the borrower (credit, income, business), NOT by the face value. The policy is a backstop, not the basis. LegalClarity: "Credit approval required: The lender evaluates your creditworthiness through a standard application process, unlike a policy loan where no credit check is needed." The lender's claim is capped at the outstanding loan balance.

**Result: no lender found — insurer, bank, or third-party — that lends against face value while the insured is alive and healthy.** The most aggressive lender in the market caps at 95% of *guaranteed cash value* and says no to the death benefit in its own FAQ.

## Angle 3 — Collateral assignment for SBA / business loans

SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective Oct 1, 2026), Section A, Chapter 5, Paragraph C — the governing rule:

- Life insurance is **required** only when (a) the loan is NOT fully secured by discounted collateral, AND (b) the business depends on one owner (sole prop, single-member LLC, or one-owner-dependent business).
- The required amount = **the collateral shortfall** — the gap between the loan and the discounted collateral value. It does NOT increase the loan amount. It is a lender-protection requirement the borrower must satisfy, at the borrower's expense (the borrower pays the premiums).
- The lender takes a collateral assignment; the assignee's claim is limited to the unpaid loan balance; the family stays beneficiary for the rest.
- Term insurance is enough — the SBA does not require whole life.

**Applied to the video:** the policy-as-collateral never creates loan proceeds. But there is a real, narrower use (see "Closest working versions"): if he builds a business with revenue and applies for an SBA 7(a) loan, a cheap $1M term policy (~$76–95/mo) can satisfy the SBA's insurance requirement when there's a collateral shortfall. The policy is a *key that unlocks the loan's paperwork*, not the money. The money comes from the business's merits + the owner's credit.

Sources: quote.planconnections.com "SBA Loan Life Insurance Requirement: SOP 50 10 Rules"; pienzawealthpartners.org; 4businessinsurance.com; SBA SOP 50 10 8.1.

## Angle 4 — Premium financing: is there ANY low-minimum product?

Tested across carrier guidelines, bank programs, and "accessible" variants. The floors are real and universal:

| Program | Minimum net worth | Minimum income | Minimum premium / face |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premium Alliance (lender checklist) | $5M (ex. primary residence) | $150,000/yr | liquid assets 10–20% of loan |
| Lincoln Financial guidelines | $5M (ages 30–70) | — | $2M face |
| Byline Bank | $5M | — | $200,000/yr premium |
| The Kuorum | $10M | $500,000/yr | — |
| Enterprise Bank & Trust | $10M | — | $500,000/yr premium |
| Carrier comparison (Aug 2026, 12 carriers) | $5M–$10M | $150k–$200k+ | $2–2.5M face / $100k yr premium |
| U.S. Bank (per Ridley Law guide) | $5–10M | — | loans typically $1–10M |

Plus: **full recourse required** (every carrier in the comparison), **outside collateral required** (stocks, bonds, CDs, letters of credit — marketable securities discounted 50%), interest paid annually, 2–5 year loan terms that must be renewed.

**The lowest bar found anywhere — Kai-Zen style leveraged IUL:** marketed as premium financing "accessible to individuals with incomes as low as $100,000 and net worth of $1M+." The structure: participant contributes ~$22,000/year for 5 years; a lender funds 2–3x more in premiums into an IUL owned through a trust; the policy is projected to pay the loan back over 12–18 years. Even this lowest bar needs $100k household income + $22k/year of durable contributions + ~$1M net worth — and it hands you no cash now; it builds a retirement policy over more than a decade. (Ridley Law's verdict on Kai-Zen: built for people with millions who can post more collateral when things go wrong; sold to regular earners it stacks two variable bets — loan rate and illustrated returns — for 15+ years.)

**Non-recourse premium finance (the historical loophole):** pre-2010 there were deals where the lender's only recourse was the policy itself. Carriers killed it: Symetra's guidelines state "Loan must be 100% collateralized at all times (non-recourse or hybrid loans will not be allowed)"; most carriers issued notices opposing non-recourse deals and STOLI; a NY Insurance Dept opinion held some premium-finance transactions lack insurable interest and are not permissible. One firm (AllFinancial Group) still advertises "non-recourse premium financing... secured only by the life insurance policy itself" — but it is for **older consumers with existing policies**, and it lends against the policy's **fair market value**. A new policy on a healthy 35-year-old has a fair market value of ~$0 (see the arithmetic below), so even this outlier lends ~$0 here.

**Result: no premium-finance product exists for zero income / zero net worth.** The lowest real floor is ~$100k income + ~$1M net worth (Kai-Zen), and traditional programs start at $5M net worth.

## Angle 5 — Single-premium / MEC / trust as borrower

- **Single-premium whole life (SPWL):** pay once, get lifelong coverage. A single premium of ~$1M+ would create immediate cash value of roughly 85–95% of the premium, borrowable at ~90% — so yes, you *can* borrow ~$1M against a policy on day one. The catch is circular: you need the ~$1.1M first. From zero capital this is not a loophole, it is a restatement of the problem.
- **MEC (Modified Endowment Contract):** a single premium usually makes the policy a MEC, which changes the tax treatment (loans taxed as income first, plus 10% penalty before age 59½). There are ways to design a single-pay policy as non-MEC, but that does not change the capital requirement.
- **Trust as owner/borrower:** the owner of the policy is the borrower — IBLOC states "The insurance policy owner must be the borrower." So if the trust owns the policy, the trust can borrow. This changes WHO borrows, not HOW MUCH: the cap is still 90–95% of cash surrender value. In an ILIT, the insured still cannot borrow (incidents of ownership), but the trustee borrowing against the policy's cash value is a different act — and it is still capped by the cash value, which is ~$0 on a new policy.
- **"Bank on Yourself" / Infinite Banking:** the design (dividend-paying whole life + paid-up additions rider) is real and accelerates early cash value — the book claims up to 40x more year-1 cash value than a traditional policy (Pamela Yellen's claim, not independently verified here). Even at the best PUA-heavy design, year-1 cash value is 56–85% of premiums *paid* — at $100/mo, ~$670–$1,020. The mechanism is real; the scale is the problem, not the mechanism.

**Result: trust ownership changes the borrower, not the borrowing base. Single premium works instantly only if you already have the money.**

Sources: singlepremiumplans.com; hansgoldstein.com "Be Your Own Bank" (max-funded IUL: $20,000 premium over 2 years → ~$14,231 cash surrender value); seedtime.com Bank On Yourself review; biggerpockets.com IBC thread; bancorplending IBLOC brochure.

## Angle 6 — Pseudo-legal "strawman / EIN / trust fund" theories: tested, not just labelled

The video's EIN + trust step is adjacent to sovereign-citizen "redemption" / strawman theory, which claims a secret Treasury account exists for every citizen, reachable via the EIN/trust/birth certificate. Tested against primary sources:

- **IRS, "The Truth About Frivolous Tax Arguments":** the notion of secret accounts assigned to each citizen "is pure fantasy."
- **IRS Notice 2010-33** lists as frivolous: the straw man argument, "redemption" by drawing on a straw man account, and the claim that putting all assets in a trust avoids income tax while keeping control.
- **Penalties:** $5,000 per frivolous return or submission (26 U.S.C. § 6702); up to $25,000 Tax Court penalty for frivolous arguments (§ 6673).
- **Courts:** "every court that has looked at these theories has rejected them" (Ridley Law, citing United States v. Benabe, 2011 — theories "should be rejected summarily, however they are presented"; McLaughlin v. CitiMortgage, 2010 — redemption theories "rejected by federal courts for at least 25 years").
- **FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin (2011):** believers think each citizen has a Treasury account worth $630,000 to $3M+; the accounts do not exist.
- **Criminal exposure:** acting on it — paying debts with bogus bonds/sight drafts drawn on the nonexistent account — has led to fraud, forgery, and fictitious-instrument charges; the scheme's originator (Roger Elvick) and numerous promoters were convicted and imprisoned.
- **No statute, no regulation, no lender, no mechanism** supports accessing money via an EIN/trust alone. The EIN is a tax identifier; a trust is a legal container. Neither is a source of funds.

**Result: tested and closed. There is no legal mechanism here — and using the pseudo-legal version carries real criminal and financial risk. This is the one angle where "find a way outside our understanding" runs into a wall that is also a cliff: the only people who get paid are the ones selling the trust package/course ($2,497 for one creator storefront course, per Ridley Law).**

Sources: ridleylawoffices.com "Sovereign Citizen Trust Scams Explained" (Sept 2026) and guide PDF; observed.org "Strawman and Redemption Theory"; en.wikipedia.org "Redemption movement"; IRS Notice 2010-33; 26 U.S.C. §§ 6702, 6673.

## Angle 7 — The closest WORKING version, from zero income and zero capital

Ranked by how much cash it actually produces, and how soon:

### 1. Income first, then PUA-heavy policy (the known best version — unchanged)
$100/mo into a PUA-heavy whole life (~$50–85k face): year-1 max loan ~$500–920; year 5 ~$3,150–$5,400; year 10 ~$8,000–$13,500. Requires $100/mo of real income first. The policy is a savings tool, not a funding source.

### 2. SBA loan with the policy as the insurance key (new from this hunt)
Build a business with revenue → apply for an SBA 7(a) loan (underwritten on the business + personal credit) → the $1M term policy (~$76–95/mo) satisfies the SBA's life-insurance requirement when there is a collateral shortfall. The policy does not add a dollar to the loan amount, but it can be the document that lets a borderline loan through. Cash obtained = whatever the business justifies borrowing, not $1M from the policy.

### 3. Business credit via EIN (new from this hunt — real, small, slow)
EIN (free, 10 minutes) + LLC + D-U-N-S number + business bank account + net-30 vendor accounts + secured business card (deposit = limit). This builds a business credit file over 6–12 months. Realistic early limits: hundreds to low thousands of dollars. Nearly every small business card still requires a personal guarantee and pulls personal credit for the first couple of years. A trust (not an LLC/corp) is worse for this — business credit bureaus expect an operating entity. This is a genuine $0-start credit ladder, but it leads to hundreds, not $1M, and it takes months of on-time payments.

### 4. Kai-Zen style leveraged IUL — the lowest premium-finance floor found
Needs ~$100k household income + ~$22,000/year contributions for 5 years + ~$1M net worth. No cash now; policy projected to retire the loan in 12–18 years. Out of reach from zero, but it is the lowest bar in the entire premium-finance universe — if income ever reaches $100k, this is the first door that opens, and it still takes 5 years of contributions before the leverage is fully in place.

### 5. The "instant $1M" version that actually works — single premium
Put ~$1.1M into a single-premium policy, borrow ~$1M against it the same week. Requires having $1.1M. Circular from zero, but it is the only design where the video's timeline (borrow $1M immediately) is literally true — and it proves the point: the money has to come from somewhere, and the policy only stores it.

### 6. Face value → cash while alive, the only real mechanisms — age or illness only
- **Life settlement:** sell the policy for 10–25% of face (typically ~4x cash surrender value). Requires age 65+, $100k+ face, 2+ years in force. A 35-year-old does not qualify, and a new policy does not qualify.
- **Viatical settlement:** no age minimum, but requires life expectancy under 24 months. Not a plan; a tragedy.
- **Accelerated death benefit:** terminal (death within 12–24 months, physician-certified), chronic (unable to perform 2 of 6 ADLs for 90+ days), or critical illness (heart attack, stroke, cancer, etc.). A healthy person cannot trigger it.

### 7. The arithmetic that explains ALL of it (my calculation, assumptions stated)
The market value of a $1M policy on a healthy 35-year-old to any third-party buyer:
- Present value of the $1M death benefit, discounted 5% over ~45 years of life expectancy: ~$111,000.
- Present value of the premiums the buyer must pay (~$1,000/mo × ~45 years, discounted 5%): ~$214,000.
- Net value to a buyer: ~**negative $103,000** — before the buyer's profit margin.
That is why no settlement buyer, lender, or premium-finance desk will pay anything for a new policy on a young healthy person: the policy is worth less than nothing to them. It is not a rule or a conspiracy; it is the time value of money minus the cost of keeping the policy alive. The only way the number turns positive is if the insured is old or sick (short horizon, high mortality) or if real cash has already been paid in (cash value).

## Angle 8 — A different reading of the video: does the "loan" come from somewhere else?

Tested: bank, credit union, private/hard-money lender, SBA, third-party policy lender, settlement buyer, premium-finance lender.

- **Bank/credit union personal loan:** underwritten on the borrower's credit + income. A policy assigned as collateral is a *plus factor*, not the basis. From zero income + damaged credit, no.
- **Private/hard-money lender:** lends against real collateral (real estate) at 65–75% LTV, or against a business's receivables. A policy with $0 cash value is not collateral they accept at any LTV.
- **The one reading where the video is closest to true:** the video may be a garbled version of the *infinite banking* pitch (real mechanism, wrong timeline) mixed with the *collateral assignment* concept (real, but the loan is underwritten on the borrower). In both real versions, the money comes from premiums already paid or from the borrower's own creditworthiness — never from the face value itself.
- **One more possibility tested — "the trust borrows, not the person":** a trust can be the borrower (see Angle 1), and a trust-owned policy's owner is the borrower (IBLOC). But the borrowing base is still cash surrender value. Changing the borrower does not change the collateral.

**Result: no alternative source of the "loan" exists. Every possible lender values the same thing — cash value, outside collateral, income, or proximity to death — and the video's setup has none of the four.**

## The grain of truth (what the video gets right)

- Policy loans are real, need no credit check, and are tax-free while the policy stays in force.
- Collateral assignment is real, and a policy can secure a bank/SBA loan.
- Trusts and EINs are real legal tools; an ILIT really does own policies for wealthy families.
- Premium financing is real — for people with $5–10M.
- "Be your own bank" is a real *savings* discipline — you recycle your own premiums through policy loans instead of paying a bank's interest.
- The video stitches five real tools into a sequence that skips the one step every tool requires: **money in first.** It is not that the tools are fake; it is that the sequence starts at step 3 and pretends steps 1–2 don't exist.

## Claude independent check (Nov 21, 2026 — Gravity-Studio issue #30)

Owner's ask: find ANY legal way a trust + EIN + new $1M policy yields large cash. I checked the ground independently, starting from the law rather than from Atlas's sources.

1. **The legal ceiling is set by statute, not by lenders.** NY Insurance Law §4222: the loan value of a policy "shall be the cash surrender value at the end of the current policy year" (insurer may defer a loan up to six months). Other states follow the same standard non-forfeiture model. So the insurer itself cannot lend more than cash surrender value, whoever owns the policy (person, trust, LLC with an EIN). The trust/EIN changes the *borrower*, never the *collateral*. Source: https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/ISC/4222
2. **Third-party lenders don't beat the statute.** Insurance-backed lines of credit (e.g. The Bancorp's IBLOC) are marketed as lending against the policy's cash value; I found no lender lending against a living insured's death benefit. Agrees with Atlas Angle 2.
3. **My own arithmetic, $100/mo budget ($1,200/yr):** that buys roughly $100k–$130k of whole-life face, not $1M (a $1M base policy for a healthy 35-year-old runs ~$9k–$14k/yr — consistent with the labelled $700–$1,210/mo range above). With a PUA-heavy design, year-1 cash value is typically ~60–80% of premium ≈ $700–$960; at a 90% loan ratio that is **~$650–$860 borrowable in year 1**. Atlas's $500–$920 range holds.
4. **Two open nits from Atlas, settled:** (a) premium — use one labelled range everywhere: **$700–$1,210/mo** for a $1M whole-life base policy (spans both sources; the $900–$1,150 range sits inside it); (b) Kai-Zen floor — the triple-check (`maximum-loans-from-zero-2026-11-21.md` A2) corrected it to **$200k household income**; INBOX now says so.

**Verdict:** concur with Atlas, independently. No legal path turns a new $1M face amount into $1M of cash for a living, healthy insured with no income or capital. Mill rating ✖ for money-now stands on two independent checks. The working path is the one in Angle 7: income first (services lanes), then a PUA-heavy policy sized to that income; SBA lending with cheap term insurance as the key-person requirement; and an EIN business-credit file built slowly. Any step that tells a lender the face value is collateral is misrepresentation — not built.
