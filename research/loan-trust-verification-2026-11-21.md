# Loan + Trust video — claim tested with real numbers

**Claim (sirenpatriot X video, ~1.5 min, transcribed Nov 21, 2026):** buy a $1M whole life policy (~$100/mo), get an EIN from the IRS, create a trust with it, put the policy in the trust, borrow $1M against the policy — buy a house/car cash, pay off debt, "be your own bank."

**Verdict: REFUTED on every load-bearing assumption. $0 accessible now. 0 of 6 conditions needed for the $1M-now version are true.**

## 1. Policy loans — REFUTED (they borrow against cash value, not death benefit)

- A policy loan borrows against the policy's **cash value**, never the death benefit. The death benefit is only paid when the insured dies; the insurer will not advance it while the insured is alive.
- Maximum loan: **75–90% of cash surrender value** in most contracts. Sources: hettlerinsurance.com (Oct 2026) — "Most insurers cap policy loans at between 75% and 90% of your available cash value"; legalclarity.org — ceiling "generally falls between 90% and 95% of the cash surrender value"; BetterWealth 2026 carrier comparison — Guardian's ceiling ~90% of cash value; Arkansas insurance regulation 23 CAR §93-305 — "at least 75% of the policy's cash surrender value may be borrowed"; US government life insurance (38 CFR §6.13) — max 94% of cash value.
- Policy loan interest: typically **5–8%** (hettlerinsurance; legalclarity). Unpaid interest capitalizes into the loan. If loan + interest ever exceeds cash value, the policy lapses — and the outstanding loan balance above cost basis becomes **taxable income** (hettlerinsurance).
- "Pay yourself back with interest" is misleading: you pay interest to the **insurer**, not yourself. The cash value keeps growing only because the insurer lends its own money secured by your cash value; your cash value was never removed.

## 2. Cash value growth, $1M whole life — the timeline kills the claim

| Year | Cash value, $1M whole life |

| 1 | **$0** (Investopedia case study: age 25, $1,562/mo premium — year 1 cash value $0; MassMutual 1980 data: level-premium policy year-1 cash value $398 on $5,910 premium = 6.7%) |
| 5 | **~$9,000–$37,000** (termvswholelifeinsurance $500k table scaled ×2: ~$9,200; Investopedia $100k chart scaled ×10: ~$37,400) |
| 10 | **~$37,000–$116,000** (same two scaled illustrations) |
| 20 | **~$125,000–$338,000** (termvswholelifeinsurance $1M page, age 35, $870/mo, top-5 mutuals: $170,000–$235,000; Investopedia $100k chart scaled: ~$338,000) |
| 30 | **~$290,000–$500,000** (termvswholelifeinsurance $1M page: $290,000–$370,000; Investopedia $1M case, age 25, $1,562/mo: $500,000) |

- Surrender charges eat most early value if you exit: year 1: 90–100%; year 5: 50–60%; year 10: 10–20%; year 15: 0% (termvswholelifeinsurance).
- Even after 20 years of paying ~$870/mo (~$209,000 in premiums), cash value is roughly $170,000–$338,000 — and the max loan is 75–90% of that: ~$128,000–$304,000. Still nowhere near $1M, after two decades.

## 3. Premium reality — REFUTED ($100/mo is ~9–12x too low)

$1M whole life, healthy male ~35, non-smoker, monthly premium (2026 sources):
- $870/mo — termvswholelifeinsurance.com, top-5 mutuals (Northwestern Mutual, MassMutual, Guardian, NY Life, Penn Mutual)
- $952/mo — MoneyGeek, Sept 2026, men, average health
- $1,210/mo — WealthNation, Oct 2026, one mutual's base whole life product
- $701/mo — Annuity Expert, preferred class
- $800/mo — Mozdex
- $640–$1,020/mo — Silver State Life, ages 35–40
- **Consensus: ~$700–$1,210/mo; typical ~$870–$950/mo.**

What $100/mo actually buys:
- **~$78,000–$86,000 of whole life** (MoneyGeek: $116/mo per $100k for men 35; WealthNation: $128/mo per $100k).
- $100/mo **does** buy $1M of 30-year TERM life ($95/mo termvswholelifeinsurance; $76/mo Mozdex standard) — but term has **zero cash value and cannot be borrowed against**. The video almost certainly conflates term pricing with whole-life borrowing. That is the sleight of hand.

## 4. Fast cash-value (PUA-heavy "infinite banking") designs — VERIFIED as real, but they don't change the math

- A properly designed banking policy minimizes base premium and maximizes paid-up additions (PUAs). Year-1 cash value as a share of premium paid:
  - 56% — usawealthbuilder calculator ($14,000 of $25,000 first premium)
  - 60–70% of contributions in years 1–3 — insurancegeek.com
  - 75–85% — BetterWealth 2026 carrier files (Penn Mutual, MassMutual, Security Mutual)
  - 70–90% accessible in year 1 — IBC design guides; wealthformula: "typical to advance 70–80% of the initial premium after 30 days"
- PUA load fee: 4–9% (insuranceandestates). Break-even (cash value = total premiums paid): year 4–7 (insurancegeek), year 5+ (BetterWealth), year 13–15 on conservative assumptions (usawealthbuilder).
- Hard cap: the 7-pay / MEC test — overfund past the IRS limit and the policy becomes a Modified Endowment Contract; loans are then taxed as distributions, not tax-free loans (legalclarity; IRC §7702A).
- **The killer math:** even at 90% year-1 efficiency, cash value ≈ 90% of what you PAID. To have $1M cash value in year 1, you must pay ~$1.1M in premiums in year 1. At $100/mo ($1,200/yr), the best design gives year-1 cash value ≈ $670–$1,020, and a max loan of ≈ **$500–$920**. The design changes the timeline by years, not the order of magnitude.
- "If someone tells you infinite banking produces positive results in year one, they're either running misleading illustrations or they don't understand the product." — insurancegeek.com

## 5. Trust angle — REFUTED (the trust makes borrowing harder, not easier)

- **ILIT (irrevocable life insurance trust)** — the only trust type that makes sense for holding life insurance: "You cannot borrow against a policy owned by an ILIT because the insured is not the policy's owner" (Absolute Trust Counsel). "The insured cannot borrow against the ILIT policy. That transaction would be tantamount to retaining 'incidents of ownership.' The Trustee can take out a loan, but if the loan benefits the insured in some way, the beneficiary could sue the trustee" (Lambert PLC). "The creator of the ILIT cannot borrow against the cash value of the life insurance policy... cannot receive back what has already been paid into the trust" (Nash & Kromash). Ridley Law lists as an ILIT drawback: "You can't change the trust, take the policy back, or borrow against it."
- **Revocable living trust:** changes nothing about borrowing capacity — the trust owns the property, not you, and "most lenders are reluctant to make loans on assets that they cannot seize" (The Nest). "It is virtually impossible to borrow money from an irrevocable trust."
- **What an ILIT is actually for:** estate tax. It keeps the death benefit out of the taxable estate (IRC §2042). The 2026 federal exemption is $15M per individual — the video's audience has no estate-tax problem. Plus a 3-year lookback (IRC §2035) if you transfer an existing policy in.
- **EIN:** real and free — IRS.gov: "get an EIN directly from the IRS in minutes for free... we'll issue your EIN immediately online. You never have to pay a fee." An EIN is just a tax ID number. It creates no money and no borrowing power.

## 6. Lender angle — REFUTED (collateral = cash surrender value, never face value)

- "The lender will use the net cash surrender value — not the face amount — as the basis for the loan amount. Expect a lender to loan 70–90% of the available cash value" (Crestmont Capital, COLI/collateral-assignment loans).
- Cash-value lines of credit lend "often 90–95% of [cash value]" (insuranceandestates) — 90–95% of cash value, not of face value.
- "The lender underwrites you like any other borrower. Credit checks, income verification, and formal underwriting are all part of the process. The lender is not obligated to make the loan just because the asset exists" (LegalClarity).
- Collateral assignment is mostly used for SBA/business loans — and there the policy is usually TERM (cheapest way to satisfy the lender's death-benefit requirement), because the lender's real protection is the death benefit if the borrower dies, not a loan against face value (Money.com).
- No legitimate lender lends $1M against a policy with ~$0 cash value. The face value is not collateral while the insured is alive.

## 7. Premium financing — VERIFIED as real, but gated to the wealthy

- Real: a bank lends you money to pay premiums on a large permanent policy (usually $5M–$100M+ death benefit) so you don't liquidate assets.
- Requirements: net worth **$5M–$10M+** (theagentsoffice: $5–10M+; Enterprise Bank: $10M minimum + $500,000 annual premium; Premium Alliance checklist: net worth >$5M excluding primary residence, $150,000+ annual income if employed, liquid assets 10–20% of the loan amount, 2 years tax returns, standard-or-better health class). Target market: $5M+ estate, $100,000+/yr premiums (jamesburnslaw).
- Collateral is the borrower's **outside personal assets** (stocks, bonds, real estate), reduced by the policy's growing cash value — not the policy's face value.
- Risks are real: variable rates (SOFR/Prime + spread); when the spread inverts, collateral calls follow. Case cited: $1.8M collateral call due in 30 days on a $25M policy (theagentsoffice). Aronson v. Brave Strategies: $150M death benefit, collateral demands ballooning from $1.5M to millions.
- A broke person with damaged credit and no income cannot access premium financing. And the loan pays premiums — it does not hand you cash to buy a house.

## 8. Best LEGITIMATE version for a broke person (no/low documented income, damaged credit)

**Gate 1 — can he even get a $1M policy? Almost certainly not.**
- Financial underwriting: coverage must be justified by income, typically **10–20x annual income** (HelpAdvisor: ~10x salary; insurancebyheroes: 20–30x by age; a 35-year-old earning $200k can justify $3–5M). At ~$0 documented income, justifiable coverage is ~$0. A $1M application would likely be declined at financial underwriting before medical underwriting.
- $1M also requires full medical underwriting: paramedical exam, blood/urine, physician statement (termvswholelifeinsurance $1M page).
- Premium $870–$950/mo is unaffordable on no income.
- Damaged credit is NOT the binding constraint on a policy loan — insurer policy loans require **no credit check** (hettlerinsurance; BetterWealth; LegalClarity). The binding constraints are income justification and premium affordability.

**Gate 2 — what $100/mo actually builds (calculated from sourced rates, not a carrier illustration):**
- Policy: ~$80–85k whole life, PUA-heavy cashflow design.
- Year 1 ($1,200 paid): cash value ≈ $670–$1,020 (56–85% first-year efficiency) → max loan ≈ **$500–$920**, available 10–45 days after funding (BetterWealth 2026 carrier comparison).
- Year 5 ($6,000 paid): cash value ≈ $4,200–$6,000 (break-even ~year 5) → max loan ≈ **$3,150–$5,400**.
- Year 10 ($12,000 paid, rough extrapolation): cash value ≈ $11,000–$15,000 → max loan ≈ **$8,000–$13,500**.
- To borrow $1M: need ~$1.1–1.3M cash value (at 75–90% loan-to-value) → requires paying ~$1.1–1.7M in premiums over decades, or a single premium of ~$1M+.

**Honest bottom line:** life insurance is a wealth-storage tool for people who already have money, not a money-creation tool for people who don't. The fastest real path to borrow meaningful money via life insurance is: (1) get income first (the money lanes already built — GEO audit, concierge, dead-lead reactivation, Missed-Call Rescue); (2) then fund a PUA-heavy policy at whatever is affordable — $100/mo gets ~$500–900 borrowable after year 1, ~$3–5k after year 5. There is no version of this that produces $1M now, soon, or without capital.

## What would have to be true for the video's $1M-now version to work

1. Insurers lend against the death benefit, not cash value — FALSE (75–90% of cash surrender value is the cap, by contract and state law).
2. A $1M whole life policy costs ~$100/mo — FALSE ($700–$1,210/mo; $100/mo buys ~$80k whole life, or $1M of term with $0 cash value).
3. A new policy has ~$1M cash value on day one — FALSE ($0 traditional; 56–90% of premiums paid in the best PUA design).
4. A trust increases borrowing power — FALSE (an ILIT blocks the insured from borrowing entirely; a revocable trust changes nothing).
5. A lender accepts face value as collateral — FALSE (net cash surrender value is the basis; 70–95% LTV on cash value).
6. Financial underwriting is waived — FALSE (10–20x income justification; $1M needs ~$50–100k+ documented income plus a medical exam).

**0 of 6 are true.**
