## Ashwagandha, Honestly: The "Smell of the Horse" Root, From Ayurveda to Today's Trials

Ashwagandha is everywhere right now: in gummies, in coffee blends, in social media routines that promise calm by Friday. Behind the trend is something much older and much quieter, a root that Indian medicine has kept in its pantry for a very long time.

This post keeps the two kinds of knowledge apart. First, what the tradition actually recorded. Then, what modern studies have tested. And last, who should not take it at all, because that list is longer than most labels admit.

## What it is

Ashwagandha is the root of *Withania somnifera*, a small shrub in the nightshade family. The Sanskrit name tells you how it was first known: the root smells like a horse. One review of the Ayurvedic record puts it this way: "The root smells like horse ('ashwa'), that is why it is called Ashwagandha." The same line adds the old promise attached to the name, that it gives the strength of a horse.

**[TRADITIONAL]** In Ayurveda, ashwagandha is a *rasayana*, a rejuvenating preparation. Singh and colleagues' 2011 review of its classical uses, which draws on the Charaka Samhita, defines rasayana as a preparation "that promotes a youthful state of physical and mental health." The tradition recorded it in a few everyday forms:

- **Churna (powder).** The review describes ashwagandha as "commonly available as a churna, a fine sieved powder that can be mixed with water, ghee (clarified butter) or honey."
- **With milk.** The classical uses section records that "when given with milk, it is the best tonic for children." That is a record of history, not advice to copy for a child today.
- **As a fermented tonic.** Ashwagandharishta, a traditional preparation made from the root, was used for nervous complaints such as anxiety and poor memory.

The US National Institutes of Health notes the same lineage from the other side: the root "has been used in the traditional Ayurvedic and Unani medicine systems of India as an adaptogen."

So in its home tradition, ashwagandha is a slow, daily tonic, not a quick fix.

## What the research has tested

**[PROVEN — small, short human trials]** Unlike many traditional herbs, ashwagandha has been tested in people, mostly for stress, anxiety and sleep. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (fact sheet updated May 2025) summarises it like this:

- **Stress and anxiety.** A 2021 systematic review found seven studies with 491 adults, all in India, lasting 6 to 8 weeks, using 240 to 1,250 mg a day of root extract. Benefits "appeared to be greater with doses of 500 to 600 mg/day."
- **Sleep.** A 2021 meta-analysis of five studies with 372 adults, lasting 6 to 12 weeks, found the benefits "more prominent when the dose was 600 mg/day and when the treatment duration was at least 8 weeks."

The overall verdict is careful: ashwagandha extracts "may help reduce anxiety and stress and improve sleep," but "it is challenging to develop recommendations" because studies used different preparations and doses. The 2011 Ayurveda review reaches the same place from the tradition's side: "Large scale clinical studies are needed to prove the clinical efficacy of this herb."

One more thing matters for a pantry herb. The trials used concentrated extracts, not the plain dried root. A spoonful of root powder in warm milk is the traditional food form, not the studied dose.

## Using it the traditional way

- **Buy the root, and know what you have.** Look for *Withania somnifera* root on the label. Cut-and-sifted root is for simmering; fine powder is the churna form.
- **The kitchen method.** Stir about ¼ to ½ teaspoon of root powder into a cup of warm milk (or a plant milk), with a little honey or ghee if you like, and drink it in the evening. This follows the traditional pairings; it is a kitchen method, not a tested dose.
- **Treat it as a ritual, not a rescue.** The tradition used it daily over weeks, and the trials ran 6 to 12 weeks. It is not a same-night sleep aid.
- **Don't stack forms.** If you already take an ashwagandha capsule or gummy, those are the stronger, concentrated form. Don't add the tea on top without checking with your clinician or pharmacist.
- **Take breaks.** NIH says it "appears to be well tolerated for up to about 3 months of use, but its long-term safety is not known."

## Who should skip it: read this first

**Safety is not automatic.** Both NIH and Memorial Sloan Kettering list real cautions:

- **Pregnancy.** Memorial Sloan Kettering says plainly: "Don't use ashwagandha if you're pregnant because it may increase your risk of miscarriage." France's food-safety agency advises against it when breastfeeding too.
- **Liver.** NIH reports "a few reports of more serious side effects, including adverse effects on liver function," and Memorial Sloan Kettering notes symptoms improved after people stopped taking it.
- **Thyroid.** It "might also affect thyroid function," and NIH describes case reports of an overactive thyroid that cleared after stopping.
- **Hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.** Both sources say it may not be safe, because it may raise testosterone.
- **Autoimmune disease.** Memorial Sloan Kettering warns it "may also trigger or exacerbate autoimmune diseases."
- **Medicines.** It may add to the effects of sedatives, including benzodiazepines, barbiturates and anticonvulsants, and NIH lists possible interactions with diabetes, blood-pressure and immune-suppressing medicines.
- **Rules differ by country.** NIH notes that Denmark banned ashwagandha in 2023.

If you take any regular medicine, have a thyroid, liver or autoimmune condition, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your clinician or pharmacist before using ashwagandha in any form. Do not stop or reduce a prescribed medicine in favour of it.

**[FRINGE]** You will see posts online claiming ashwagandha "cures anxiety" or "kills cancer cells." Memorial Sloan Kettering says lab studies showed effects on cancer cells but "this effect has not been observed in humans," and no source we read says it is a treatment for any disease.

## The bottom line

Ashwagandha is an old Ayurvedic tonic with a real lineage and a small, promising set of human trials on stress and sleep. It is not a cure, not a sedative and not for everyone. Used the traditional way, as a modest daily cup over a few weeks, with the safety list read first, it is a pantry herb with a long and honest story.
